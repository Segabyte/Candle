# Idea 001 — Shared near-miss & safety-signal network

**Captured:** 2026-08-22
**Status:** Raw capture — not yet scoped
**Tags:** health & safety, workplace, cross-company, prevention

---

## The idea in one line

A tool that records the small, preventable things that go wrong at work —
near misses, minor hazards, "this shouldn't have happened" moments — keeps
reminding people so it doesn't happen again, and **shares the signal beyond
the walls of a single company** so that a hazard learned in one place
prevents an injury in another.

## Where it came from

I was organising a health and safety meeting and wanted it to be genuinely
interactive rather than a presentation people sit through. I asked teammates
for the list of topics we'd covered before — deliberately *not* looking them
all up myself, because knowing every answer in advance kills the fun of the
game and makes the participation feel staged. I wanted to participate as a
presenter too, not just run it.

Two topics I remembered presenting, going back two or three years:

- **SIT** — the impact of prolonged sitting while working, and how moving more
  improves your health.
- **The 20/20/20 rule for eyesight** — about how eyesight is affected by
  looking at a monitor at arm's distance, and taking regular breaks to look
  into the distance. (I don't recall the exact numbers.)

Thinking about that today, the idea arrived: the same energy we put into
health and safety meetings and fire drills should apply to the *small* things
that actually happen day to day.

## The real examples behind it

**The coffee machine.** One day in the office I noticed the coffee machine
hadn't been switched off and the coffee left in it was heating up. Entirely
avoidable. So we put in place some simple controls — alarms, a 3 p.m. cutoff
for making coffee, turning the machine off, pouring out whatever's left.
Small, local, effective.

**The mat.** Someone trips because a mat isn't laid properly. Within the next
hour, someone else — in the same company, or a completely different one —
falls the same way. Isn't it important that we know about the first one, share
it, and make sure everybody is safe?

## Why it matters

Many unfortunate things should never happen in the first place, but they do,
and for all kinds of reasons — often nothing more than a simple mistake or
plain ignorance. And those small things can genuinely impact other people,
completely unintentionally.

The hazard is rarely unique to one building. A badly placed mat, an appliance
left on, a blocked exit — the same failure repeats everywhere. Today that
knowledge stays trapped inside whichever company happened to experience it
first. It shouldn't. If someone somewhere has already learned this the hard
way, everyone should get the benefit of that lesson.

## What it might do

- Let anyone quickly record a small incident, near miss, or "this could hurt
  someone" observation — low friction, seconds not forms.
- Keep the reminder alive so the fix sticks, rather than the report being
  filed and forgotten. (Like the coffee alarm and the 3 p.m. rule.)
- Feed the recurring health & safety meeting: real, local, recent material
  instead of generic slides.
- **Share beyond the company** — a common pool where hazards, causes and
  fixes are visible to other organisations, so a lesson learned in one place
  prevents an incident in another.
- Possibly sit as its own app, or as a module inside a broader company /
  health & safety tool.

- What makes people actually report? The barrier has to be near zero, and it
  can't feel like blame.
- How is it shared across companies without exposing anything a company would
  consider sensitive or reputationally risky? Anonymised? Aggregated?
- Who runs the shared pool — an industry body, a neutral operator, a product?
- How do you keep it from becoming noise, so a relevant hazard actually
  reaches the right person at the right time?
- Where does this overlap with existing formal incident-reporting obligations,
  and where is it deliberately lighter-weight than them?
- Is the "meeting engagement" angle (interactive, game-like, participatory) a
  separate idea, or part of the same product?
